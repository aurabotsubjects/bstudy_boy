## 1. HOW TO USE THIS DOCUMENT

This is the **content blueprint** for the app/website you're building. It contains:
- The full 10-week structure (reading allocation, questions, projects, quizzes)
- NZ Curriculum links for every lesson plan
- The exact **app behaviour spec** (what happens on each day type)
- A full library of **AI image-generation prompts** (for ChatGPT/DALL·E) so the site looks amazing for 9–12 year-olds
- A spec for the **"Print All" booklet button**
- A spec for the **student workbook** (one booklet of every page students write on, printed once at the start of the topic) and the **term reading schedule** printable — both now built (Section 7)

Chapter names below are taken directly from the book's contents page, so you (or the app) can match them to whatever edition/page numbers your class set uses. *Boy*'s chapters are longer and more self-contained than a typical junior novel's (roughly 3–14 pages each), so most reading days cover one whole chapter; the five longest chapters (*The bicycle and the sweet-shop*, *Mr Coombes*, *Mrs Pratchett's revenge*, *The magic island*, *The Matron*, *A drive in the motor-car*, *Captain Hardcastle*, *Goodbye school*) are split across two or more days at a sensible pause point, marked "Part 1 / Part 2" etc. below. The teacher should find the nearest natural paragraph break to the suggested split when reading from their own edition.

⚠️ **Content note for teachers:** *Boy* is a memoir of English boarding-school life in the 1920s–30s and is frequently studied at this age level, but it contains several scenes of corporal punishment (caning) presented matter-of-factly, as was normal at the time. These scenes are historically important to the book's meaning (Dahl himself uses them to question the adults' authority and, later, his own faith), but teachers should preview **"Mrs Pratchett's revenge," "Captain Hardcastle," "Boazers,"** and **"The Headmaster"** before teaching them, and may wish to frame a short class discussion about how attitudes to discipline have changed since the 1920s. None of the discussion questions or quiz answers below ask students to describe the physical punishment in detail; they focus on cause, effect, character and how Dahl felt about it.

⚠️ **Copyright note:** Do not ask ChatGPT/DALL·E to recreate the actual book cover, Quentin Blake's illustration style (it is instantly recognisable and still in copyright), or a likeness of Roald Dahl or any other named real person. All image prompts below use original, symbolic imagery (gobstoppers, tuck-boxes, fjords, motorbikes) inspired by the book's themes rather than copying its copyrighted art.

## 2. NZ CURRICULUM ALIGNMENT (Level 3–4 English, Years 5–6)

Every lesson in this unit draws on these achievement objectives:

**Listening, Reading, and Viewing**
- *Ideas:* Show understanding of ideas within, across, and beyond texts.
- *Structure and Language:* Show understanding of how texts are shaped for different purposes and audiences (this unit is a strong entry point for teaching **memoir/autobiography** as a genre, and for discussing why Dahl insists *"This is not an autobiography"* on the dedication page).
- *Processes and Strategies:* Integrate sources of information, processes, and strategies confidently to identify, form, and express ideas.

**Speaking, Writing, and Presenting**
- *Ideas:* Select, form, and communicate ideas on a range of topics.
- *Structure and Language:* Show understanding of language features (hyperbole, comic dialogue, physical description of characters), using them appropriately.
- *Processes and Strategies:* Select and use processes and strategies to communicate confidently.

**Key Competencies:** Thinking, Managing Self, Relating to Others, Participating & Contributing
**Values embedded throughout:** resilience, courage, curiosity, questioning authority fairly, understanding how the past shaped the present
**Cross-curricular links:** Social Sciences (life in Britain and Norway in the 1920s–30s, change over time); Health & PE (how adults' responsibilities to keep children safe have changed)

## 3. THE WEEKLY RHYTHM (applies every week, Weeks 1–9)

| Day | Mode | App Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Monday** | Teacher reads aloud | Slideshow/PowerPoint-style screen: one screen per chunk (cover slide → lead-in questions → read-aloud slide with page range → comprehension/inference questions → final "go-away" reflection prompt). Teacher drives with next/back arrows. |
| **Tuesday** | Group reading (groups of 4, take turns reading) | Same content, but delivered as a **worksheet mode**: toggle between on-screen interactive (type answers into app) and **printable PDF** version. Timer/turn-tracker optional for group reading. |
| **Wednesday** | Teacher reads aloud | Same as Monday format, next reading chunk. |
| **Thursday** | Group reading | Same as Tuesday format, next reading chunk. |
| **Friday** | **Quiz day** | 20-question comprehensive quiz covering that week's four reading chunks. Auto-marked in-app; printable version with answer key for teacher. |

Every lesson (Mon–Thu) = **45 minutes**, structured as:
1. Settle & lead-in questions (5 min)
2. Reading (15–20 min)
3. Comprehension/inference questions — discussion or written (15 min)
4. Go-away reflection prompt, recorded in reading journal (5 min)

Each week also has **one rotating menu of 4 projects** (poster / comic strip / drama-acting scene / journal reflection) tied to that week's events. Groups/individuals can be assigned one, or choose — teacher's call. These are done in spare/extension time across the week, not inside the 45-minute reading lesson.

### WEEK 1 — Family, Home, and the Sweet-Shop

**Learning Intention:** We can understand how a memoir writer selects vivid, memorable details to stand in for whole years of a life.
**Success Criteria:** I can explain why the author remembers almost nothing about his early childhood except a few vivid moments; I can identify an example of exaggeration (hyperbole) Dahl uses for comic effect.

**Monday — Teacher read:** *Starting-point → Papa and Mama*
- Lead-in: Ask a grandparent or older family member about something surprising or funny that happened to them when they were young. What's one thing you already know about your own family history?
- Comprehension/Inference: Roald Dahl's father lost his arm in a shocking accident as a teenager — what actually went wrong, and why couldn't doctors fix it properly in 1877? What does his father's "glorious walks" theory (about beautiful scenery making unborn babies grow up loving beauty) tell us about the kind of person he was?
- Go-away prompt: Write 3 sentences about an object, skill, or belief that has been passed down in your own family, the way Dahl's father passed down his love of beautiful things.

**Tuesday — Group read:** *Kindergarten, 1922–3*
- Lead-in: What is your very first memory? Why do you think we remember so little from when we were very small?
- Comprehension/Inference: Two tragedies hit the Dahl family within weeks of each other — what were they, and how did Roald's mother respond? Why do you think the author remembers his tricycle rides so clearly but almost nothing else about his first year of school?
- Go-away prompt: Describe the bravest thing you know that a member of your own family has ever done, the way Dahl's mother stayed and raised five children alone.

**Wednesday — Teacher read:** *The bicycle and the sweet-shop* (Part 1: to the end of the backpedalling-bicycle memory)
- Lead-in: Has a much older kid ever done something that seemed impossibly cool to you? What was it?
- Comprehension/Inference: Why did the image of the older boy on his bicycle stay in Dahl's memory for sixty years? What does this tell us about what nine-year-olds find important?
- Go-away prompt: Describe your own "one glorious day I will…" ambition — something you've watched someone else do that you desperately want to be able to do yourself.

**Thursday — Group read:** *The bicycle and the sweet-shop* (Part 2: Thwaites, the sweets, and Mrs Pratchett)
- Lead-in: What is the most disgusting food rumour you've ever heard from a friend at school? Did you believe it?
- Comprehension/Inference: Why did Thwaites's story about Liquorice Bootlaces work on the boys even though it was clearly ridiculous? Find two details Dahl uses to make Mrs Pratchett seem thoroughly unpleasant.
- Go-away prompt: Invent your own outrageous (and clearly false) explanation for how a sweet or snack is made, in the style of Thwaites.

**Weekly Project Menu (choose or assign one):**
1. **Poster:** Design the front window display of Mrs Pratchett's sweet-shop, illustrating at least 5 sweets named in the chapter (Gobstoppers, Bootlaces, Sherbet Suckers, Pear Drops, Tonsil Ticklers) with your own made-up prices.
2. **Comic strip:** 6-panel comic of Thwaites explaining how Liquorice Bootlaces are made, ending with the other boys' reactions.
3. **Drama/acting scene:** Act out young Roald and his friends peering through the sweet-shop window deciding what to buy with their sixpence — no money in reality, all mime and dialogue.
4. **Journal reflection:** Write a diary entry as nine-year-old Roald on the day he first saw the boy backpedalling on his bike.

**Friday Quiz 1 — 20 Questions (Teacher answer key)**
1. What country was Roald Dahl's father, Harald Dahl, originally from? — *Norway.*
2. How did Harald Dahl lose his left arm? — *He broke it falling off a roof as a teenager; a drunken doctor made it worse pulling on it, and it had to be amputated.*
3. What business did Harald Dahl set up in Cardiff? — *A shipbroking firm (supplying coal and other goods to ships), with his partner Aadnesen.*
4. What was Harald Dahl's theory about "the glorious walks"? — *That taking a pregnant woman to beautiful places would make her unborn baby grow up loving beautiful things.*
5. What two family tragedies happened within weeks of each other when Roald was three? — *His sister Astri died of appendicitis, and then his father died of pneumonia.*
6. What did Roald's mother decide to do instead of returning to Norway with her children? — *Stay and raise them in Wales/England, honouring her husband's wish for an English education.*
7. What was the name of the kindergarten Roald attended? — *Elmtree House.*
8. What form of transport does Roald remember most vividly from kindergarten? — *His tricycle.*
9. What school did Roald attend from age 7 to 9? — *Llandaff Cathedral School.*
10. What amazing thing did the older boy on the bicycle do that impressed young Roald so much? — *He backpedalled at speed with no hands on the handlebars.*
11. Who was Thwaites, and why did the other boys trust his opinions? — *A friend whose father was a doctor, so he claimed medical authority on everything.*
12. According to Thwaites, what were Liquorice Bootlaces really made from? — *Rats (boiled-up "rat-mash").*
13. What disease did Thwaites claim you would catch from eating Liquorice Bootlaces? — *"Ratitis."*
14. According to Thwaites, why did Gobstoppers change colour as you sucked them? — *"It's your spit that does it."*
15. What did Thwaites claim was inside Tonsil Ticklers, and why? — *Chloroform, supposedly put there to keep dangerous prisoners quiet.*
16. What was the name of the woman who ran the sweet-shop? — *Mrs Pratchett.*
17. Name two things Dahl says made Mrs Pratchett so unpleasant. — *Accept any two: her filthy hands, her rudeness, her meanness with bags, her sour expression.*
18. Inference: Why do you think the boys kept believing Thwaites's outrageous stories even though they didn't stop buying the sweets? — *Accept reasoned answers: the stories were entertaining/exciting, or being a doctor's son made him seem believable.*
19. Vocabulary: What does it mean that Mrs Pratchett was "loathsome"? — *Very unpleasant/disgusting; hateful.*
20. Short answer: In your own words, why do you think Dahl remembers so few things from before he was eight or nine, but remembers this sweet-shop scene in perfect detail? — *Open answer, mark for understanding that vivid/exciting or emotional moments stick in memory more than ordinary ones.*

### WEEK 2 — The Great Mouse Plot and Its Consequences

**Learning Intention:** We can explain how tension and humour build together in a personal narrative, and track cause and effect through a chain of events.
**Success Criteria:** I can retell, in order, the chain of events from finding the mouse to the identity parade; I can explain how Thwaites's scaremongering builds suspense.

**Monday — Teacher read:** *The Great Mouse Plot*
- Lead-in: Have you or a friend ever come up with a "brilliant" plan that felt genius at the time? How did it turn out?
- Comprehension/Inference: Whose idea was the Mouse Plot, and how does Dahl want us to feel about him for owning up to it as an adult? Why does Dahl insist "one must strive to be truthful… Truth is more important than modesty" right before describing his own plan?
- Go-away prompt: Write 3 sentences describing a moment when you (or someone you know) felt like "a hero" for pulling off a plan, even a naughty one.

**Tuesday — Group read:** *Mr Coombes* (Part 1: the closed shop → arriving at prayers)
- Lead-in: Imagine you did something wrong and then something unexpected and scary happened afterwards that seemed connected. How would you feel?
- Comprehension/Inference: What do the boys see through the sweet-shop window that alarms them? Why does Thwaites's claim about heart attacks terrify young Roald so completely?
- Go-away prompt: Describe a time you worried you'd caused something far worse than you actually had.

**Wednesday — Teacher read:** *Mr Coombes* (Part 2: the playground line-up → Mrs Pratchett identifies the five boys)
- Lead-in: How do you think it would feel to be picked out of a line-up of one hundred people?
- Comprehension/Inference: Describe how Mr Coombes organises the "identity parade." Why is the boys' relief that Mrs Pratchett is alive followed immediately by fresh dread?
- Go-away prompt: Write the playground scene from Mrs Pratchett's point of view as she walks the lines looking for "them nasty little ones."

**Thursday — Group read:** *Mrs Pratchett's revenge* (Part 1: the study → all five boys have been caned)
- Lead-in: Why do you think adults in the past sometimes believed punishing children in front of others taught a stronger lesson?
- Comprehension/Inference: How does Dahl describe Mr Coombes and the cane to make the scene feel frightening even though he doesn't describe the punishment itself in graphic detail? Why is Mrs Pratchett's presence in the room, cheering the Headmaster on, especially shocking to the boys?
- Go-away prompt: Without describing violence, write 3–4 sentences about how you imagine Roald felt walking back to class afterwards.

**Weekly Project Menu:**
1. **Poster:** Design a "WANTED" poster for Mrs Pratchett's shop, listing (humorously) the "crimes" the boys accused her of.
2. **Comic strip:** 6-panel comic showing the Mouse Plot from start (finding the mouse) to the moment the boys realise the shop is closed the next morning.
3. **Drama/acting scene:** Act out the playground identity parade in groups, with one student as Mr Coombes, one as Mrs Pratchett, and the rest as nervous boys in line — freeze-frame the moment she points.
4. **Journal reflection:** Write a letter of apology from adult Roald Dahl to Mrs Pratchett, looking back on the Mouse Plot years later — is he really sorry?

**Friday Quiz 2 — 20 Questions**
1. Where did the boys find the dead mouse? — *Under a loose floorboard in their secret hiding place at the back of the classroom.*
2. Whose idea was it to put the mouse in a sweet jar? — *Roald's (the narrator's) own idea.*
3. Which jar did they decide to put the mouse in, and why? — *The Gobstopper jar, because it was out on the counter and used often.*
4. What job did Thwaites do while the mouse was being hidden in the jar? — *He distracted Mrs Pratchett by buying a Sherbet Sucker and a Bootlace.*
5. What did the boys find when they arrived at the shop the next morning? — *It was closed, with a notice on the door.*
6. What could the boys see through the window? — *The Gobstopper jar smashed on the floor, gobstoppers everywhere, and the dead mouse.*
7. According to Thwaites, what might happen to an old person who gets "a very big shock"? — *A heart attack, and death within seconds.*
8. Who did Thwaites blame for "killing" Mrs Pratchett? — *Roald (the narrator), because it was his idea and he put the mouse in.*
9. Who was the Headmaster of Llandaff Cathedral School? — *Mr Coombes.*
10. What order did Mr Coombes give the whole school that morning? — *To line up around the playground in silence.*
11. What turned out to be true about Mrs Pratchett, to the boys' great relief? — *She was alive and had come to the school with Mr Coombes.*
12. What was Mr Coombes and Mrs Pratchett doing as they walked down the lines of boys? — *Inspecting/looking for the boys who played the trick, like an identity parade.*
13. Who did Mrs Pratchett identify first? — *Thwaites.*
14. How many boys in total were identified and punished? — *Five.*
15. What object did Mr Coombes hold when the five boys were called to his study? — *A long yellow cane.*
16. Who watched and cheered during the punishment in the study? — *Mrs Pratchett.*
17. Who discovered what had happened to Roald later that evening? — *His mother, at bathtime.*
18. What did Roald's mother do that same evening after finding out? — *She marched out of the house to confront Mr Coombes.*
19. What did Mr Coombes tell Roald's mother when she complained? — *That she was "a foreigner" and didn't understand how British schools were run.*
20. Inference/short answer: In your own words, why do you think Roald's mother's response mattered so much to the story, even though it didn't change what had already happened? — *Open answer, mark for understanding that it shows her courage and her different (Norwegian) values about how children should be treated, and leads to the decision to send him to boarding school in England.*

### WEEK 3 — A Mother's Courage and Idyllic Norway

**Learning Intention:** We can compare our own lives to a time and place very different from our own, using evidence from the text.
**Success Criteria:** I can describe what the Dahl family's summer holidays in Norway were like; I can explain why Dahl calls Tjöme "the magic island."

**Monday — Teacher read:** *Mrs Pratchett's revenge* (Part 2: bathtime discovery → the decision to change schools)
- Lead-in: Has an adult ever stood up for you when you felt you couldn't stand up for yourself? What happened?
- Comprehension/Inference: How does Roald's mother react when she discovers what happened, compared to how Mr Coombes expects a parent to react? What big decision does she make about Roald's education because of this?
- Go-away prompt: Write about a time an adult's actions made you feel protected or proud of them.

**Tuesday — Group read:** *Going to Norway*
- Lead-in: What is the longest journey you've ever taken to get somewhere for a holiday? What made it feel like an adventure?
- Comprehension/Inference: List the different stages of the Dahl family's journey to Norway. Why do you think Dahl describes the "skaal" toasting ceremony and the enormous feast in such loving detail?
- Go-away prompt: Describe a family tradition of your own (a food, a game, a saying) that an outsider might find strange but that you love.

**Wednesday — Teacher read:** *The magic island* (Part 1: arrival at Tjöme → the description of breakfast)
- Lead-in: What would your idea of the "perfect" holiday spot look like?
- Comprehension/Inference: What details does Dahl use to make Tjöme sound magical despite the very basic hotel and outdoor toilets? Why might very primitive conditions not spoil a holiday for a child?
- Go-away prompt: Design (in words) your own "dream hotel breakfast table," the way Dahl describes the fifty dishes on offer.

**Thursday — Group read:** *The magic island* (Part 2: the boats → fishing in the evenings)
- Lead-in: Do you think children today are given as much freedom and responsibility as the Dahl children had in their boats? Why or why not?
- Comprehension/Inference: Compare the row-boat and the motor-boat sections — what changes as the children get older? What does Dahl's mother's calm skill in rough seas tell us about her character?
- Go-away prompt: Write 3–4 sentences about the most adventurous or free you have ever felt outdoors.

**Weekly Project Menu:**
1. **Poster:** Design a travel poster advertising a summer holiday to Tjöme, using details from the text (the fjord, the islands, the breakfast, the fishing).
2. **Comic strip:** Illustrate the Dahl family's four-day journey from Wales to Norway as a comic-strip map with 6 key stops.
3. **Drama/acting scene:** Act out a "skaal" toasting dinner scene at Bestemama and Bestepapa's house, including at least one formal toast.
4. **Journal reflection:** Write a postcard home from young Roald describing a day on the magic island.

**Friday Quiz 3 — 20 Questions**
1. What decision did Roald's mother make about his schooling by the end of "Mrs Pratchett's revenge"? — *To send him to an English boarding school once the school year finished.*
2. What did Mr Coombes accuse Roald's mother of not understanding? — *How British/English schools are run (he calls her "a foreigner").*
3. Where did the Dahl family spend every summer holiday from 1920 to 1932? — *Norway.*
4. Roughly how long did the journey to Norway take? — *About four days each way.*
5. Who in the travelling party got seasick on every crossing? — *Everyone except the mother (and Nanny hated the boat most of all).*
6. What were the names of Roald's Norwegian grandparents? — *Bestemama and Bestepapa.*
7. What was the centrepiece of the grand welcome feast in Oslo? — *A huge poached flounder (fish).*
8. What was special about the ice-cream served at the feast? — *It had crunchy bits of burnt toffee (krokan) mixed through it.*
9. What is a "skaal"? — *A formal Norwegian toasting ceremony where you raise your glass and look someone in the eye.*
10. What was the name of the island the family stayed on for their Norwegian holiday? — *Tjöme.*
11. What were the toilets like at the hotel on Tjöme? — *A wooden outhouse with a hole cut in a plank over a pit.*
12. What did Nanny keep saying she was afraid the boat would do? — *Sink/leak.*
13. What kind of boat did the family use in the early years, and who rowed it? — *A row-boat, rowed by the mother and the older half-brother.*
14. What replaced the row-boat in later years? — *A small motor-boat with an unreliable one-cylinder engine.*
15. What did the family use as bait for evening fishing? — *Mussels collected from the rocks.*
16. Inference: Why do you think Dahl describes his mother as "enjoying herself most" when the sea got rough? — *Accept reasoned answers: she was confident, adventurous, and skilled at handling the boat.*
17. Inference: Why might the very basic conditions at the hotel not have bothered the children at all? — *Accept reasoned answers: children focus on fun and adventure, not comfort; it was normal to them.*
18. Vocabulary: What does Dahl mean when he describes the island as having "absolute peace and beauty"? — *A feeling of complete calm and natural loveliness.*
19. True or False: The Dahl children learned to swim early because there were no sandy beaches, only deep water off the rocks. — *True.*
20. Short answer: In your own words, explain why Dahl calls this chapter "The magic island." — *Open answer, mark for reference to the freedom, natural beauty, and happy memories described.*

### WEEK 4 — Off to Boarding School

**Learning Intention:** We can infer a character's feelings from their actions and the details a memoir writer chooses to include.
**Success Criteria:** I can describe how young Roald felt on his first day at St Peter's, using evidence from the text; I can explain the purpose of the tuck-box and the letter-writing rule.

**Monday — Teacher read:** *A visit to the doctor*
- Lead-in: Has a visit to the doctor or dentist ever turned out to be much more uncomfortable than you expected?
- Comprehension/Inference: What operation does eight-year-old Roald undergo, and how does the author make clear it was done without anaesthetic? What does his grandmother's comment at the end suggest about what she has learned in her long life?
- Go-away prompt: Write about a time you had to be braver than you expected to be during a medical or dental visit.

**Tuesday — Group read:** *First day*
- Lead-in: Imagine arriving somewhere completely new where you don't know a single person and will be staying for weeks. What would worry you most?
- Comprehension/Inference: What is a tuck-box, and why did English boarding schools of the time rely on parents to send food parcels? How does Dahl describe the Headmaster to make him seem intimidating from the very first handshake?
- Go-away prompt: Write a short diary entry as nine-year-old Roald on the evening of his very first day at St Peter's.

**Wednesday — Teacher read:** *Writing home*
- Lead-in: If you could only write to your family once a week and knew a teacher was reading every word before it was sent, what would you choose to say?
- Comprehension/Inference: Why weren't the boys allowed to complain about the school in their letters home? How did the Headmaster cleverly stop the boys from correcting their spelling mistakes, and why did this help hide the fact that letters were censored?
- Go-away prompt: Write the opening two sentences of a "perfectly cheerful" letter home that secretly hides how you're really feeling — then explain to a partner what you really meant.

**Thursday — Group read:** *The Matron* (Part 1: introduction of the Matron → the sugar-on-the-corridor prank and its punishment)
- Lead-in: What's the strictest rule you've ever had to follow, and what happened if someone broke it?
- Comprehension/Inference: What makes the Matron such a frightening figure to the boys? How does the whole school end up being punished for one boy's prank, and how does Arkle's loyalty to his pet frog show his character?
- Go-away prompt: Describe a time the whole class or team was punished for what one person did. Was it fair?

**Weekly Project Menu:**
1. **Poster:** Design the label/contents chart for your own imaginary tuck-box, listing at least 6 items you'd want sent from home.
2. **Comic strip:** 6-panel comic of the letter-writing scene, showing the Headmaster catching a "mistake" and the boy's true feelings in a thought bubble he can't say out loud.
3. **Drama/acting scene:** Perform the moment young Roald is left alone on the driveway on his first day, showing his feelings without words (mime), then act out putting on a brave face for the Headmaster.
4. **Journal reflection:** Write a truthful letter home from Roald that he would never have dared to actually send.

**Friday Quiz 4 — 20 Questions**
1. What was removed from young Roald's mouth by the doctor in Norway? — *His adenoids.*
2. Was Roald given anaesthetic for this operation? — *No.*
3. What did Roald's grandmother say to him after the operation? — *That it won't be the last time he goes to a doctor in his life, and hopefully they won't do him too much harm.*
4. What was the name of Roald's first boarding school? — *St Peter's, in Weston-super-Mare, Somerset.*
5. How did Roald and his mother travel from Cardiff to Weston-super-Mare? — *By paddle-steamer across the Bristol Channel.*
6. What is a tuck-box? — *A small locked wooden trunk for storing food and treasures sent from home; private to its owner.*
7. Name two things typically found inside a tuck-box. — *Accept any two: cake, biscuits, fruit, chocolate, sweets, or small treasures like a penknife, magnet, or toy.*
8. What unusual pet did a boy called Arkle keep inside his tuck-box? — *A frog, fed on slugs.*
9. What memorable detail does Dahl give about the Headmaster's appearance when they first meet? — *His gold-edged front tooth and heavily creamed, glistening hair (and his "shark's grin").*
10. What instruction does the Headmaster give Roald on his very first minute at the school? — *To go and report to the Matron.*
11. When was letter-writing time at St Peter's? — *Sunday morning, from nine o'clock for one hour.*
12. Why couldn't boys complain about the school in their letters home? — *The Headmaster patrolled and read every letter as it was written.*
13. How did the Headmaster stop boys from correcting mistakes in a way parents might notice? — *He made them leave the mistake in the letter and write corrections out separately as a punishment, so the letter looked unedited.*

20. Short answer: In your own words, describe how the boy we met in Week 1 (nervous, homesick, mischievous) has changed by the end of the book, and give one piece of evidence to support your answer. — *Open answer, mark for a clear before/after comparison using specific text evidence.*

## 6. WEEK 10 — FINAL PROJECT & CELEBRATION (no new reading)

**Learning Intention:** We can synthesise our understanding of a whole text into an original creative or informative piece of work, and present it clearly to an audience.
**Success Criteria:** I can choose and complete a final project that shows deep understanding of *Boy: Tales of Childhood*; I can present my work with confidence.

**Structure over the week (3 lessons):**
- **Lesson 1:** Introduce the 4 final project options (below) and rubric. Students choose individually, in pairs, or in small groups. Planning time.
- **Lesson 2:** Independent/group work time, with teacher check-ins and a printable progress checklist.
- **Lesson 3 (or a separate celebration afternoon):** Presentations/gallery walk, "Choose Kind"-style class discussion on what we learned about resilience, courage and how times have changed, and awarding of certificates.

**Final Project Choices (choose one):**
1. **"My Own Tales of Childhood" memoir chapter:** Write and illustrate one true, vivid chapter from your own life so far, in Dahl's style — a single memorable event told with humour and detail (not a whole autobiography).
2. **Then-and-Now report:** Research and present (poster, slideshow or short written report) how school discipline, medical treatment, or travel has changed between the 1920s and today, using at least 3 specific examples from the book.
3. **Mini "Boy" museum exhibit:** Design a small museum display (physical or digital) of 5–6 "artefacts" from the book (a tuck-box, a gobstopper jar, a cane — described respectfully as a historical object, a paddle-steamer ticket, a Cadbury testing box) each with an information card explaining its significance.
4. **Dramatic scene compilation:** In a group, choose and rehearse 3 short scenes from across the book (comedy encouraged, no violence performed) to perform for the class or at the celebration, with a short spoken introduction explaining why you chose each scene.

**Rubric (printable):** Understanding of the text (evidence and detail) / Creativity and effort / Clarity of presentation / Use of NZC-aligned language features (e.g. hyperbole, characterisation, memoir voice).

**Certificate:** Printable "Boy Book Study — Completed!" certificate template for every student.

## 7. APP BEHAVIOUR SPEC (Build Guide)

- One "🖨️ Print week pack" button triggers `window.print()`; "Save as PDF" in the print dialog gives the teacher a single PDF containing the whole week's prep in one document, rather than printing each day separately.
- **Future work:** extend the same pattern to a full-term "Print All" button once Weeks 2–10 are built, adding a generated table of contents and the Week 10 final project brief + rubric + certificate, per the original spec below.

**📊 Reading Achievement Rubric (whole-programme) — IMPLEMENTED:**
- A **"📊 Rubric"** link is always visible in the top navigation bar, from any screen, since this rubric spans the entire ten-week programme rather than living inside one week.
- The rubric is a fixed set of 8 whole-programme criteria derived from Section 2's NZC achievement objectives, split into Reading and Writing strands plus two Key Competencies:
  1. **Reading — Ideas:** Explains ideas within, across and beyond the text (e.g. why Dahl chooses to remember certain moments).
  2. **Reading — Structure & Language:** Identifies how the memoir is shaped for its purpose, incl. hyperbole and comic exaggeration.
  3. **Reading — Processes & Strategies:** Uses evidence from the text to infer feelings, motives or unstated meaning.
  4. **Writing — Ideas:** Selects and communicates own ideas clearly in go-away reflections and journal writing.
  5. **Writing — Structure & Language:** Uses language features (hyperbole, dialogue, vivid detail) appropriately in own writing.
  6. **Writing — Processes & Strategies:** Plans, drafts and shares writing or project work with growing independence.
  7. **Key Competency — Thinking:** Asks questions and forms a reasoned opinion about fairness or authority in the text.
  8. **Key Competency — Participating & Contributing:** Contributes constructively during group reading discussions.
- The teacher adds student names via a text box; each student becomes a column in a grid, with the 8 criteria as rows.
- Clicking a cell **highlights it and records the date** the student demonstrated that skill — a simple click-to-toggle "achieved" mark, exactly as requested, rather than a numeric score. Data is saved to the browser's local storage on that device only (no server/back end), so it persists between visits on the same computer.
- A **"🖨️ Print blank rubric"** button prints a paper version of the same grid for teachers who prefer to tick achievement by hand.
- **Rubric checkpoints inside lesson plans:** every day's printable NZC lesson plan (see above) carries a highlighted **"📊 Rubric checkpoint"** box naming which 1–2 rubric criteria to watch for that day, with a plain-language description of what to look/listen for, plus small tags showing the criterion code(s). For Week 1 these are:
  - **Monday** (Papa and Mama): R1 (Reading — Ideas), R7 (Thinking) — watch for students explaining why the arm-accident and "glorious walks" details were chosen, meaningful memories, and who question whether the father's parenting idea was fair or strange.
  - **Tuesday** (Kindergarten): R3 (Processes & Strategies), R8 (Participating & Contributing) — watch for students inferring why the mother's response shows courage, and contributing constructively in the group.
  - **Wednesday** (Bicycle & sweet-shop pt 1): R1, R4 (Writing — Ideas) — watch for students explaining why the bicycle memory has stuck for sixty years, and writing a clear go-away reflection.
  - **Thursday** (Bicycle & sweet-shop pt 2): R2 (Reading — Structure & Language), R5 (Writing — Structure & Language) — watch for students identifying Dahl's hyperbole about Mrs Pratchett, and using similarly vivid exaggeration in their own invented sweet-story.
  - **Friday** (Quiz): R6 (Writing/Processes — independent recall) — use quiz results as the checkpoint, and record which students need more support before Week 2.
- **Future work:** as Weeks 2–10 are built, each new week's days should get their own `rubricIds` + `rubricNote` following this same pattern, so the rubric's "Checkpoints" column (e.g. "Wk1 Mon/Wed · Wk3 · Wk9") is populated with real, working links once those weeks exist. The rubric itself does not need to change — it already covers the whole programme.

**📅 Term Reading Schedule (printable) — IMPLEMENTED (whole term, Weeks 1–9 + Week 10 note):**
- A **"🖨️ Print term reading schedule"** button sits on the landing page, under the "Ten weeks, one memoir" shelf. It opens a standalone printable page (`nav('readingplan')`, rendered by `renderReadingPlan()`).
- **Covers the whole term now**, not only built weeks. Data lives in a `READING_PLAN` array (one entry per week 1–9, each with 4 rows for Mon–Thu: `[who, chapter title, optional Part start→stop note]`, where `who` is `T` = teacher read or `G` = group read). It is kept separate from the `WEEK1`-style week data so Weeks 2–9 appear before they are built. **Source of truth = the Monday–Thursday lines in Section 5;** if a chapter or split point changes there, update `READING_PLAN` too.
- Layout: **two A4 pages** (Weeks 1–5, then Weeks 6–10). One table per page with columns **Day | What we read | Read by | Pages | Done**. Each week starts with a navy heading row showing the week number and short theme (from `WEEK_THEMES`) with a "Friday: quiz" tag on the right. Week 10 shows a single "No new reading — time to finish, present and celebrate" row.
- **Pages** is left as `pp. ____ – ____` because page numbers differ between editions; students or the teacher fill it in from their own class copy. **Done** is a tick box.
- Built by the shared `wbSchedule()` function, so the standalone page and the workbook pages are identical. The 5 + 5 week split was tested to fit exactly two A4 pages; if rows are lengthened, re-check that it still fits.
- ⚠️ Stop markers are copied from Section 5 and include the wording "all five boys have been caned" (Week 2 Thursday) and "the punishment" (Week 6 Thursday). Consider softening these for student-facing print (see the content note in Section 1).

**📘 Student Workbook (printable booklet) — IMPLEMENTED (Week 1):**
- A **"📘 Build student workbook"** button sits beside "Start Week 1" on the landing page hero (`nav('workbook')`, rendered by `renderWorkbook()`). The screen explains what is included so far, has a **"🖨️ Print workbook"** button plus print tips, and shows an on-screen preview of every page.
- **Purpose:** the teacher prints one booklet at the start of the topic so nothing needs printing again. It replaces the loose "reading journal" that the go-away prompt refers to — students write everything in the workbook and keep it for the term.
- **Contents, in order:**
  1. **Cover** — full-bleed A4 cover using the supplied artwork (see Section 8, G). Text overlaid: "BOY / Tales of Childhood / Student Workbook" in the parchment scroll, and a "NAME" label above the writing line on the luggage-tag label.
  2. **Welcome page** — how the workbook works (3 parts of each reading day, Friday quiz, project menu), a contents list generated from the weeks included (plus the reading schedule), and name/class lines.
  3. **Term reading schedule** — the two pages described above.
  4. **For each built week:**
     - **Week overview page** — the week's Learning Intention and Success Criteria. Each success criterion has a self-check row (☐ Not yet ☐ Getting there ☐ Got it!) — an addition not in the original plan. Also the **spare-time project menu** (4 options with tick boxes) and 5 lines for a "My project plan".
     - **One page per reading day, Mon–Thu**, identical for teacher-read and group-read days: chapter heading, **Lead-in** (3 writing lines per question), **Comprehension & inference** (3 lines per question), **Go-away reflection** (6 lines).
     - **Friday quiz** — student copy with name/date line; multiple choice shown as ○ a) – d), short answers with 2 lines. Flows over about 3 pages; questions never split across pages.
- **Left out on purpose (teacher-only):** lesson plans, answer keys, rubric, NZC alignment block and the teacher content note.
- **Size:** about 8 pages per week (1 overview + 4 days + ~3 quiz) plus 4 fixed pages (cover, welcome, 2 schedule pages). Week 1 alone = 12 pages, which suits double-sided printing.
- **How it grows:** `wbWeeks()` returns the list of weeks to include. When a week is built, add one entry — e.g. `{n:2, theme:WEEK_THEMES[1], data:WEEK2, quiz:QUIZ2}` — and its pages, the welcome-page contents and the "covers N of 10 weeks" footer all update automatically. For this to work, each new week's data must follow the `WEEK1` shape: `li`, `sc`, `days[]` (each with `key`, `label`, `mode`, `chapter`, and for reading days `leadIn[]`, `comp[]`, `goAway`), and `projects[]` (`icon`, `title`, `text`); and the quiz must follow the `QUIZ1` shape (`q`, `type` = `mc`/`short`, `opts`, `a`, `model`). **Write `sc` as criteria separated by semicolons** — the workbook splits on them to make one self-check row per criterion.
- **Print technical notes:** A4 size; the page rules (`@page`) are injected only while the workbook or reading-schedule screen is showing, so every other print job in the app is unchanged. The first page has zero margin so the cover bleeds to the edge; other pages have 14 mm margins and a page number in the bottom margin (Chrome/Edge; other browsers may omit page numbers). Each section starts a new page (`break-before: page`). The cover is a real `<img>` rather than a CSS background, so it prints even when "Background graphics" is off. Recommended settings: A4, double-sided (flip on long edge), headers and footers off. "Save as PDF" makes a file for a print room.
- **Future work — workbook pages to add as the app grows:** Weeks 2–9 (via `wbWeeks()`); **Week 10** final project brief (4 options), student project tracker/checklist and presentation-day schedule; the **"Boy Book Study — Completed!" certificate** as the last page.
